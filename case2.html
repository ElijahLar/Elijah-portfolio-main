EloEcho

A solo concept prototype for clearer League coaching

EloEcho is a solo concept prototype for clearer League of Legends coaching. I designed it around one question: after a ranked loss, how can a player understand the most important reason they lost without being buried in stats?

This case explores a landing page, a speculative live overlay, and a companion dashboard built around one core principle: less feedback, but better feedback. The current version uses mocked and redacted match examples to show the coaching flow clearly. It is not yet a live Riot-integrated product.

My Role and Scope

I defined the product direction, wrote the framing, designed the landing page, shaped the live overlay concept, and built the companion dashboard layout and feedback hierarchy. I used Codex to speed up parts of the prototype build, then edited and tightened the result myself.

The value of the project is not that it is fully shipped. It is that it shows how I think through product scope, feedback design, interface hierarchy, and tradeoffs across multiple surfaces.

Design Challenge

A lot of gaming tools show huge amounts of data, but that does not always help the player understand what actually went wrong. League players often leave a loss with numbers, graphs, and timelines, but still no clear sense of what they should fix first.

The design question became: how might I reduce post-match analysis into one useful takeaway and one next action while the game is still fresh in the player's mind?

Too much data, not enough direction

Players often get plenty of numbers but not a clear read on which mistake mattered most.

Post-game learning fades fast

If feedback comes too late or feels too abstract, it is easy to queue again without learning anything useful.

Coaching needs focus

The strongest version of this product is not about saying everything. It is about surfacing one clear thing to fix while the game is still fresh.

Website Direction

The site leads with a very direct promise: stop guessing why you lost. I wanted the product to feel specific from the first screen, not like another generic AI helper or stat tracker.

Visually, I leaned into contrast, atmosphere, and a more serious competitive tone. The goal was to make it feel native to the game space while keeping the layout readable enough that the product message stayed clear.

Live Read and In-Game Feedback

I explored the live overlay as a speculative direction for faster feedback. During or around the match, the player sees a lightweight read of what is happening: champion, timing, core stats, warnings, and one coaching note that points to the most relevant problem.

The idea is interesting, but it also comes with real product questions around distraction, usefulness, and feasibility. Because of that, I see the overlay as the riskier direction and the post-game companion as the stronger product path, where the feedback can be more reflective and less disruptive.

Companion App and Command Deck

The companion view pushes the idea further by gathering match context into one command deck. It shows key stats, the current read on the player, an enemy dossier, lightweight rules, and a coaching panel that turns the analysis into one practical instruction.

This became the strongest part of the concept for me. Instead of acting like a full analysis suite, the interface tries to connect raw match information to one coaching takeaway, which makes it feel more purposeful than a standard stat tracker.

Key Design Decisions

One takeaway over a stat dump

I prioritized a single coaching read instead of trying to summarize everything that happened in the match.

Separate live feedback from review

I split the concept across a live overlay and a companion view so the player is not asked to process too much in the middle of play.

Command-deck layout for fast scanning

I used boxed panels, short labels, and clear hierarchy because this product needs fast reading more than long-form explanation.

What Changed During the Process

The main shift was moving away from the idea of giving the player more information and toward giving them less, but better. Early on, the concept risked becoming another dense analysis tool. The stronger direction was reducing everything into one useful next action.

That decision affected the whole case: the website became sharper, the overlay became lighter, and the companion dashboard became more about prioritization than completeness.

Reflection

EloEcho earns its place in the portfolio because it shows product thinking, interface direction, and information hierarchy in a space that is easy to overdesign. The challenge was not adding more features. It was deciding what to leave out so the player gets one clear read instead of a wall of analysis.

For recruiters, I think the strongest part of the case is the judgment behind it: choosing one useful takeaway over more data, separating live feedback from post-game review, and shaping a concept that feels specific instead of generic.
